Penalty for Online Blackmail in Saudi Arabia

This guide explains Penalty for Online Blackmail in Saudi Arabia in plain language, with the practical steps anyone facing this situation in Saudi Arabia needs to know.

This guide explains the penalty for online blackmail in Saudi Arabia under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, with practical steps for responding correctly if you are a victim of this crime.

Online blackmail is a crime strictly punished under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, covering any threat to publish photos, information, or private conversations in exchange for money, benefit, or coercing the victim into a specific act. The law doesn't distinguish based on the perpetrator's or victim's nationality or geographic location, as long as the crime occurred or had effect within the Kingdom.

The Statutory Penalty for Online Blackmail

The penalty for online blackmail reaches imprisonment and a substantial financial fine, escalating if the blackmail aims to force the victim to commit an unlawful act, if the victim is a minor, or if the perpetrator repeats the act. The victim is also entitled to claim compensation for material and moral damages alongside the criminal case.

The First Rule: Don't Pay and Don't Respond to the Threat

Responding to the blackmailer's demands, whether by paying money or fulfilling another request, usually doesn't stop the blackmail but encourages more of it, as it proves to the blackmailer the threat is effective. The golden rule in these situations: don't pay, don't delete conversations or evidence, and don't suddenly cut off contact before consulting a specialist.

Correct Documentation Steps Before Reporting

Every message or conversation containing the threat must be documented, with screenshots showing the date, time, and the blackmailer's number or account if possible. This documentation is the primary evidence the competent authorities build their investigation on, and it's preferable not to delete anything even if it seems embarrassing, as confidentiality in handling these reports is statutorily guaranteed.

Confidentiality in Handling Blackmail Reports

The competent authorities handle online blackmail reports with complete confidentiality preserving the victim's privacy and reputation, an established principle aimed at encouraging victims to report without fear of exposure or social embarrassment.

What If the Blackmailer Is Outside the Kingdom?

The blackmailer being outside Saudi Arabia doesn't prevent legal prosecution; there are international cooperation channels and technical tracing tools reaching many perpetrators even outside the country's borders, and the competent authorities assess each case individually to determine the best prosecution route.

When You Need a Specialized Lawyer

Immediate contact with a lawyer specialized in these cases helps you manage the situation correctly from the first moment, from guiding you on correct documentation to following the report and criminal prosecution through to the perpetrator's arrest and accountability.

Documents and Evidence Needed for the Report

Filing the report needs: documented screenshots of conversations or messages containing the threat, and any information about the blackmailer's account or number if available. No need to delete anything before complete documentation, and no additional documents are needed at the urgent initial consultation stage.

Common Question: Can Offensive Content Already Published Be Removed?

Yes, removal of published content can be requested through competent channels alongside filing the criminal report, and speed of documentation before any deletion attempt by the perpetrator themselves is critically important.

Conclusion: You're Not Alone in Facing This Situation

Online blackmail is a psychologically exhausting experience, but Saudi law provides real protection and complete confidentiality for victims. Acting correctly from the first moment, away from fear or responding to the blackmailer's demands, is what determines successful legal prosecution later.

Practical Case: Blackmail Through a Dating App

Imagine someone who met another person through a dating app, shared personal photos in trust, then was surprised by a threat to publish them to their workplace or family unless a specific amount is paid. This pattern is very common, and the correct action: don't pay immediately, document every conversation, and report immediately without embarrassment over the nature of the relationship that started the problem; the law protects the victim regardless of the initial acquaintance circumstances.

Blackmail by a Former Partner

Another common case: a threat from a former partner to publish private content after the relationship ends. This type is treated with the same legal seriousness, and the blackmailer needn't be a stranger; the prior relationship doesn't grant either party a right to later threaten or blackmail.

The Role of Tech Companies in Tracing Perpetrators

Social media platforms and major apps cooperate with competent authorities in Saudi Arabia to provide data helping identify blackmailers, especially in well-documented cases with clear evidence. This cooperation increases the chances of reaching the perpetrator even if they used seemingly anonymous accounts.

Protecting Children and Teenagers from Online Blackmail

Children and teenagers are more vulnerable to becoming online blackmail victims due to their lack of experience handling these situations. We recommend parents maintain ongoing dialogue with their children about the risks of sharing photos or personal information online, and reassure them that reporting any threat won't expose them to blame, as fear of parental reaction often prevents children from seeking help early.

Steps After the Perpetrator Is Arrested

After the blackmailer is arrested and referred to the Public Prosecution, the victim can follow the case's progress and claim due compensation within the same criminal proceedings without needing to file a separate civil case.
